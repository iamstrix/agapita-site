Agapita

A voice for stroke survivors who cannot speak

Agapita helps people with Broca's aphasia communicate by drawing. A rough sketch becomes a clear, complete message for their nurse.

  • Works without an internet connection
  • Patient information stays inside the hospital
  • Runs on affordable tablets

Agapita is a prototype in development. It is not a medical device and has not yet been tested with patients.

The problem

After a stroke, many people understand everything around them but cannot get their words out.

This is called Broca's aphasia. Many people with it also have weakness on one side of the body, which makes fine movements hard.

Existing communication aids ask patients to tap through grids of small icons. That is slow and frustrating for someone with shaky hands. At night, with no one in the room, a patient may have no way to ask for help at all.

Agapita works the other way round: the technology adapts to the patient. A shaky circle, star or stick figure is enough.

How it helps

  1. 1

    The patient draws

    A simple sketch on a bedside tablet, with large buttons that are easy to press.

  2. 2

    Agapita suggests a message

    The sketch is turned into a clear sentence. The patient checks it and can choose a different meaning.

  3. 3

    The nurse is told

    Once the patient confirms, the message goes straight to their nurse. It can also be read aloud.

For patients

  • Communicate without speaking or spelling
  • Nothing is sent until the patient confirms it
  • More independence and dignity

For nurses and carers

  • Know what is needed before reaching the room
  • Less time spent guessing what a patient means
  • Less physical and mental strain on busy shifts

For hospitals

  • No internet connection needed
  • Patient data stays on site, in line with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173)
  • Affordable tablets instead of costly specialist devices

Impact

People

  • Restores patient agency. Stroke survivors who cannot speak can ask for what they need.
  • Reduces burnout. Nurses spend less time working out what a patient wants.
  • Widens access. Built to be affordable for public hospitals in the Philippines.

Environment

  • Gives old devices a second life. Older tablets can become bedside boards.
  • Low carbon. Runs locally rather than in energy-hungry data centres.
  • Less paper waste. Replaces letter boards and flashcards thrown away between patient stays.

Our story

Ten years ago, the founder's grandmother had a severe stroke. Years later she described the night shifts in hospital: no one in the room, no way to speak, waiting in silence until morning.

Agapita was built so that no patient has to wait like that again.

Giving a voice back to the beloved, one sketch at a time.
